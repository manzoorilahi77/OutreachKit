01

The market, and the only four numbers that matter

You are not selling to "small business." You are selling to a specific kind of operator: owner-run, revenue between roughly $150k and $2M, does physical work, and has never had a reason to sit down for two days and build a website.

US small businesses~34MRoughly 27M are non-employer, one-person or family operations
Estimated with no real site~25–30%A Facebook page, a Linktree or a dead builder page is not a site
Per mid-size metro300–900Qualified no-site prospects in a single good category, one metro
You need to close2–4 / moAt $2,000 average build + $80/mo care plan
The whole business in one line. 250 profiles sourced → 100 qualified → 100 contacted → 12–18 replies → 5–7 calls → 2–3 closes. Every number below exists to make one of those six arrows less leaky. If you close three builds a month at $2,000 and keep 70% on a $80/month care plan, you are at $72k of build revenue and roughly $20k of recurring by the end of year one.

Why "no website" is the right wedge

  • The pain is legible. You can show them the gap in ten seconds: their competitor ranks, they do not.
  • No incumbent to displace. You are not fighting an agency, a nephew, or a two-year contract.
  • The buyer is the owner. One decision-maker, no committee, no procurement.
  • It opens a recurring door. The site is the low-price entry; hosting, reviews, Maps, and ads are where the business actually lives.

Where beginners lose money

  • Chasing every category at once. Your pitch, your demo, and your search strings only get sharp inside one vertical.
  • Selling a website. Nobody wants a website. They want the phone to ring in a slow month.
  • Picking a ticket that is too small. A $200-per-job operator will not spend $1,500. Ticket size is the single hardest filter to fix later.
  • Picking a category agencies already farm. Roofing, HVAC and personal injury get pitched daily. You are the fourth call that week.
  • No recurring attached. A build-only shop restarts from zero every month, forever.

Tier A

Start here

High ticket, wide gap, and no queue of agencies. These pay for a real site, close in one or two calls, and have an obvious second and third product. Pick one and stay in it for at least 90 days.

Tier B

Workable

One factor is soft — the ticket is thinner, the vertical is busier, or the owner is harder to reach. Good as a second category once your first one is producing, or as your primary in a small market where Tier A runs dry.

Tier C

Know why you are there

Either the money is too thin, the category is agency-saturated, or a website is genuinely not what moves their revenue. Work these only for a specific reason: a warm referral network, a niche you already know, or a booking-tool sale instead of a site.

If you want the short answer. The five highest-scoring categories on the board are cabinet refinishing & painting (90), fencing & gates (88), commercial janitorial (87), parking-lot striping & sealcoating (86) and artificial turf install (86), with concrete & stamped driveways and epoxy floor coatings a point behind. Big enough jobs to pay you, deeply under-webbed, visual enough that a portfolio obviously helps, and almost nobody is pitching them. If you would rather sell recurring revenue than one-off builds, start with commercial janitorial, daycare & preschool or martial arts schools — lower build prices, far higher lifetime value.

05

Filtering — who is actually a lead

Two passes. The first asks whether they have a real website. The second asks whether they can pay you and whether you can reach them. Both should take under a minute per business.

Pass one — what "no real website" looks like

Hard signals — treat as no website

  • The Google Business Profile shows only Directions and Call. No Website button at all. The strongest signal there is.
  • The Website button points at facebook.com, instagram.com, linktr.ee, a Yelp / Angi / Thumbtack / Houzz / Nextdoor / BBB profile, or a phone-number-only landing card.
  • The domain is a dead free host: *.business.site (Google shut these off in 2024), *.wixsite.com, *.godaddysites.com, *.square.site, *.weebly.com, *.blogspot.com, *.myshopify.com for a service trade.
  • The listing shows "Claim this business", or is unverified, or the name is misspelled — nobody is minding it.

Soft signals — a site exists but is worthless

  • Open it on your phone. Text does not reflow, buttons are too small, you have to pinch. Ten seconds decides it.
  • No phone number in the header, or the number does not tap-to-call.
  • No quote form anywhere, or a form that only asks for an email.
  • Footer says © 2016, or the last blog post is four years old.
  • No HTTPS, or a certificate warning.
  • Takes more than four seconds to paint on cellular.
  • Zero project photos, or photos that are obviously stock.

Soft-signal businesses are a better sale than no-site businesses in one way: they already believe a website is worth paying for. They just got a bad one.

Pass two — disqualify fast

×
Franchises and national brands. The website is corporate's. There is no sale here, ever.
×
Anything with 500+ reviews or 20+ trucks. They have a marketing manager and a contract.
×
Sub-$500 average job with no recurring. They physically cannot justify $1,500.
×
Fewer than 5 reviews and nothing posted in a year. The business may be dead. Verify before you spend an hour.
×
Outside your service radius by more than one metro. You want to be able to say "I'm in [their city]" and mean it.
×
An agency footer on a site that looks decent. You are pitching against an incumbent for a rebuild. Different, slower sale.
×
Every review inside the same two weeks. Bought reviews. Not a business you want your name on.

07

What the website has to look like

A local service site has one job: turn a stranger who is standing in their driveway holding a phone into a phone call. Everything below serves that. The builder in section 04 adds the parts specific to your category.

The spine — every category, in this order

01Above the fold. Service + city in the headline. One line of proof. A tap-to-call number and a "Get a free estimate" button, both visible without scrolling.
02Sticky call bar on mobile. Fixed to the bottom of the screen, always. This one element moves conversion more than the rest of the page combined.
03Trust strip. Licensed & insured, years in business, review count and star rating, warranty, financing if they offer it.
04Services grid. Four to eight, each with a real photo and one plain sentence. No jargon.
05Proof. Twelve to twenty of their own project photos. Before/after pairs where the category allows it. Never stock.
06Reviews. Pulled from Google, with names and dates. Link out to the profile so they are verifiable.
07How it works. Three or four steps from first call to finished job. This removes the fear of being sold to.
08Service area. A real list of the towns they cover, each linking to its own page. This is the SEO engine.
09FAQ. Price ranges, timeline, permits, warranty, payment. Answer the awkward ones honestly.
10Quote form. Name, phone, city, service, photo upload, best time to call. Six fields maximum.
11Footer NAP. Name, address, phone typed character-for-character the same as the Google profile.

The technical floor — non-negotiable

  • Mobile first. Most of their traffic is a phone held one-handed in a driveway.
  • Under 2.5 seconds to largest paint on a mid-range Android over cellular.
  • Images compressed to WebP and sized. Contractor photos are 8MB straight off a phone.
  • HTTPS and a domain they own, registered in their name, not yours.
  • LocalBusiness schema with hours, geo, service area and price range.
  • Analytics plus a tracked phone number so you can show them, in month two, exactly what you produced. This is what renews the care plan.
  • Readable contrast and real focus states. A meaningful share of their customers are over 60.
  • One page per service, one page per city. A single stuffed home page ranks for nothing.
Design register, by category. Trades want heavy, credible, high-contrast — big type, dense proof, phone everywhere. Personal care and wellness want calm, spacious, photographic — booking button above the fold. B2B and commercial want plain, specification-led — capabilities, certifications, a quote request form, and no stock photography at all.
08

Desktop or mobile — the actual answer

This is not a preference question. Some sources only exist properly in a phone app, and some tasks are unworkable without tabs and a keyboard. Here is the split, task by task.

TaskUseWhy
Instagram hashtag & location browsingPhone appHashtag and place browsing is app-first; the desktop web version is crippled for this. Use the Recent tab, never Top.
Nextdoor business finderPhone appNeighbourhood recommendation threads are where the truly invisible one-truck operators surface.
Facebook local & trade groupsPhone appGroup search and "recommendations" posts work far better in-app.
Checking whether their site is brokenPhone, real deviceEmulators lie about tap targets, font scaling and load time on cellular. Ten seconds on a real phone is the whole test.
Google Maps list scanningDesktopThe side panel gives you dozens of results at once and you can middle-click every promising one into a tab.
Yelp, Angi, Thumbtack, Houzz, BBB, chamber directoriesDesktopDense list views, filters, and easy copy-paste into the sheet.
State licence board & contractor registry lookupsDesktopGovernment portals are ancient. Many are effectively unusable on a phone.
Secretary of State business registrationsDesktopGives you the owner's legal name and registration date — the two facts that make a cold message land.
County permit portalsDesktopRecently pulled permits tell you exactly who is actively working, this month.
Logging leads and running the pipelineDesktopNobody sustains 50 rows a week of data entry on a phone keyboard.
Building the demo mockupDesktopObviously.
Sending the first DMPhone appInstagram and Facebook messages from a phone look like a person. From a desktop they read as automated, and are throttled harder.

The verdict

Desktop-primary, phone-in-hand. Roughly 70% desktop, 30% phone, on the same desk in the same hour. Laptop open on Maps and the sheet; phone next to it for Instagram sourcing, the mobile-broken test, and the first DM. Do not try to run this entirely from a phone — you will source well and track terribly, and untracked leads are not leads.

Should you build an app or a tool for this?

No — not until you are past roughly 200 leads a week, which is at least six months away. A Google Sheet plus a browser plus your phone handles everything in this manual. Building tooling before you have closed anything is the most common and most comfortable way to avoid selling. Revisit it when data entry genuinely becomes the bottleneck, and even then a free CRM tier will probably beat anything you build.

Tracking

One sheet, eight pipeline stages, six ratios. If a lead is not in the sheet it does not exist, and you will contact the same person twice in three weeks and look like a bot.

Pipeline stages

01Sourced — name and handle captured, not yet checked
02Qualified — scored 5+, no real site confirmed

Outreach

The message is not "do you need a website." It is a specific observation about their business, plus something you already made for them.

The demo-first method

  1. Before you contact anyone, build a one-page mockup using their real name, real photos from their Instagram or Google profile, and their real service area. Ninety minutes with a template.
  2. Put it on a subdomain: theirname.yourstudio.com.
  3. Screenshot it on a phone.
  4. Send the screenshot as the first message. You are no longer asking for their time; you are showing them something that already exists with their name on it.
  5. Take it down after 30 days if they do not respond. Say so in the message — it is honest and it creates a real deadline.

Use only images they published publicly, and take the demo down if they ask. Never imply the site is already live.

The contact sequence

Day 0Instagram or Facebook DMOne specific compliment about a recent job, one sentence on what you noticed missing, the demo screenshot, one question.
Day 2Call the published business lineMid-morning or after 5pm. Trades answer from the truck. "I sent you something on Instagram — did it come through?"
Day 5Email the public business addressSubject line is their business name and city. Link to the live demo. Include your physical address and an opt-out.
Day 9Second call, different time of dayDifferent window than day 2. Leave one voicemail, only once.
Day 14The takedown note"Taking the demo down Friday — want me to leave it up?" Highest-response message in the sequence.
Day 45NurtureSeasonal hook tied to their trade. Then quarterly. Never delete a lead who did not say no.
The filled-in version lives in the builder. Section 04 generates all of this written out for your exact category and city — fifteen opening lines, all eight messages in the sequence with the objection handlers, the AI prompt that builds the sample site, and your price bands with the minimum and maximum for each tier. This section is the method; the kit is the copy.
Stay clean. Manual browsing only — scraping Instagram, Google Maps or Yelp violates their terms and gets accounts banned. Cold email to a public business address is legal under CAN-SPAM if you include a real physical address and a working opt-out, and you honour it within ten days. Cold SMS is a different animal: TCPA and several state statutes carry per-message penalties, and most local business numbers are mobile. Do not cold-text. Call, DM, or email. Check your state's rules before you assume a business number is exempt from do-not-call.
11

The first 90 days

One category. One metro. One 60-minute block a day. Everything else is a distraction until you have three paying clients.

Week 1Pick and prove the categoryRun the builder. Verify the hashtags actually have volume and drop any under ~200 posts. Confirm the job-value range against two or three real local quotes — that number is your entire price anchor. Set up the sheet.
Weeks 2–3Build the template and two demosOne reusable template for your category, filled with real content. Two spec demos for real local businesses. This is the only week you are allowed to spend on design.
Weeks 3–6Source 200, contact 200Five 60-minute blocks a week. Fifty qualified leads a week. Send every single one a demo screenshot. Expect roughly 25–35 replies and 8–12 calls across the period.
Weeks 5–8First two closes, at a discountCharge $900–$1,200 for the first two in exchange for a filmed testimonial, photos, and permission to name them. Attach the care plan anyway — discount the build, never the recurring.
Weeks 8–12Raise the price, add the second productFull price now that you have proof in the same category and city. Sell Google Business Profile management and the review system to the clients you already have — the easiest revenue on this page.
Day 90Decide: widen or deepenSame category, next metro (your template and pitch carry over — fastest path) or same metro, adjacent category (your local proof carries over — better for referrals). Pick one. Not both.

On the numbers. Job values, no-site percentages, factor scores and funnel ratios on this page are planning estimates built from category structure and typical US pricing — they are not measured survey data for your market. Treat them as a starting hypothesis and replace each one with your own figure as soon as you have real quotes and a hundred real leads. Hashtag and search-string patterns are reliable; their post volume in your specific city is not, so verify on the first pass and cross out whatever produces nothing.

On conduct. Manual searching only. No scraping, no automated messaging, no fake reviews, no implying a demo is live. Register domains in the client's name. Everything you sell should be something you can actually deliver next Tuesday.
